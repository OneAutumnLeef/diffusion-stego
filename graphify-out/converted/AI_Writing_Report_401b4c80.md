Frequently Asked Questions

How should I interpret Turnitin's AI writing percentage and false positives?
The percentage shown in the AI writing report is the amount of qualifying text within the submission that Turnitin’s AI writing detection model determines was either likely AI-generated text from a large-language model or likely AI-generated text that was likely revised using an AI paraphrase tool or word spinner.

False positives (incorrectly flagging human-written text as AI-generated) are a possibility in AI models.

AI detection scores under 20%, which we do not surface in new reports, have a higher likelihood of false positives. To reduce the likelihood of misinterpretation, no score or highlights are attributed and are indicated with an asterisk in the report (*%).

The AI writing percentage should not be the sole basis to determine whether misconduct has occurred. The reviewer/instructor should use the percentage as a means to start a formative conversation with their student and/or use it to examine the submitted assignment in accordance with their school's policies.

What does 'qualifying text' mean?
Our model only processes qualifying text in the form of long-form writing. Long-form writing means individual sentences contained in paragraphs that make up a longer piece of written work, such as an essay, a dissertation, or an article, etc. Qualifying text that has been determined to be likely AI-generated will be highlighted in cyan in the submission, and likely AI-generated and then likely AI-paraphrased will be highlighted purple.

Non-qualifying text, such as bullet points, annotated bibliographies, etc., will not be processed and can create disparity between the submission highlights and the percentage shown.

DSCSP: Diffusion-Stego with Conditional Sampling Paths for Robust Image Steganography
Authors Omitted for Review



Abstract—We introduce DSCSP (Diffusion-Stego with Condi- tional Sampling Paths), a novel steganographic framework that embeds secret messages into images generated by pre-trained diffusion models. Unlike prior diffusion-based steganography methods that project messages into initial noise vectors or require model fine-tuning, DSCSP employs a lightweight learned Controller network that maps payload bits to spatial perturbations applied to the output of a frozen DDIM sampling chain, effectively using the diffusion model’s sampling path as an information channel. A spatially-aligned Extractor network based on ResNet-18 recovers the hidden bits from the stego image. Both networks are trained end-to-end with a composite loss combining message reconstruction, perceptual fidelity (LPIPS), and orthogonality regularization, augmented by a curriculum of five differentiable attacks—Gaussian noise, Gaussian blur, brightness/contrast adjustment, crop-and-resize, and STE-based JPEG compression—to ensure robustness against common image transformations. On CelebA-HQ 256 × 256 face images, DSCSP achieves a 256-bit payload capacity with a clean bit error rate (BER) of 0.0000, PSNR of 36.7 dB, SSIM of 0.902, LPIPS of 0.032,
and FID of 13.7. We further demonstrate robustness against real (non-differentiable) JPEG compression (BER < 0.001 at Q ≥ 95), Gaussian noise, blur, and brightness/contrast attacks. Capacity scaling experiments show the framework generalizes from 128 to 512 bits by adjusting the per-cell encoding rate. An information-theoretic analysis frames the system as a structured additive channel and compares achieved throughput to Shannon capacity bounds. These results establish sampling-path encoding as a viable paradigm for high-capacity, high-fidelity, and robust generative steganography.
Index Terms—Steganography, diffusion models, deep learning, image security, robustness

- Introduction
Steganography, the art of hiding information within innocuous-looking cover media, has historically relied on embedding data into existing images through imperceptible pixel-level modifications [5]. With the advent of powerful generative models, a new paradigm has emerged: generative steganography, where the cover image itself is synthesized to carry the hidden message, eliminating the need for a pre- existing cover and offering inherent plausible deniability.
Diffusion models [8], [16] have recently achieved state-of- the-art image synthesis quality, making them attractive back- bones for generative steganography. Existing diffusion-based steganography approaches broadly fall into two categories. Noise-projection methods, exemplified by Diffusion-Stego [18], encode messages directly into the initial noise vector zT before running the reverse diffusion process, achieving a training- free approach but offering limited robustness against post- processing attacks. Model-adaptation methods [13], [21] fine-
tune or augment the diffusion model itself, which requires costly retraining and risks degrading the pre-trained model’s generation quality.
We propose a fundamentally different approach. DSCSP (Diffusion-Stego with Conditional Sampling Paths) treats the sampling trajectory of a frozen pre-trained diffusion model as an information channel. Rather than modifying the model weights or the initial noise, DSCSP learns a compact Controller network that maps payload bits to spatial perturbation patterns, which are applied to the output of the standard DDIM [16] deterministic sampling chain. A lightweight Extractor network recovers the hidden bits from the resulting stego image. Both networks are trained end-to-end while the diffusion model remains completely frozen, preserving its generation quality and enabling efficient training on consumer-grade hardware.
To ensure robustness against common image transformations encountered during transmission and storage, we introduce a curriculum-based adversarial training strategy that gradually exposes the system to five differentiable attack proxies— Gaussian noise, Gaussian blur, brightness/contrast adjustment, random crop-and-resize, and STE-based differentiable JPEG compression.
Our contributions are as follows:
- We introduce sampling-path encoding, a novel stegano- graphic channel for diffusion models that maps bits to learned spatial perturbation patterns applied to frozen DDIM outputs, achieving 256-bit payloads with 0.0000 BER, 36.7 dB PSNR, 0.902 SSIM, and FID 13.7 at
256 × 256 resolution.
- We propose a multi-bit spatial tiling Controller with orthogonality-regularized patterns that encodes 4 bits per spatial cell using only 20K trainable parameters, enabling memory-efficient training on a 6 GB GPU.
- We design a spatially-aligned Extractor with a ResNet- 18 backbone whose stride-32 feature map naturally aligns with the Controller’s 8 8 spatial grid, ensuring coherent bit recovery.
- We develop a differentiable attack curriculum com- prising five attacks with a three-phase training schedule that progressively builds robustness while maintaining reconstruction accuracy.
- We provide a comprehensive experimental evaluation including real (non-differentiable) JPEG robustness tests, steganalysis detection experiments with a trained ResNet- 18 classifier, capacity scaling from 128 to 512 bits, a base-

line comparison with prior methods, and an information- theoretic channel capacity analysis.
- Related Work
- Classical Image Steganography
Traditional steganography methods embed messages by modifying pixel values [5] or transform-domain coefficients of existing cover images. LSB (Least Significant Bit) re- placement [3] modifies the least significant bits of pixel values but is easily detectable by statistical steganalysis. More sophisticated approaches such as HUGO [14], WOW [9], and S-UNIWARD [10] minimize a distortion function to reduce detectability, but remain fundamentally limited by the cover image distribution.
- Deep Learning-Based Steganography
Neural network-based methods jointly learn encoding and decoding. HiDDeN [23] trains an encoder–decoder pair with a discriminator and differentiable JPEG proxy for robustness.

- Detectability and the Capacity–Undetectability Tradeoff
The perfect steganalysis detection (AUC = 1.0) highlights a fundamental tension in high-capacity steganography. The Con- troller’s spatial tiling creates structured perturbation patterns that, while imperceptible to the human eye (PSNR 36.7 dB, SSIM 0.902), are easily distinguishable by a learned classifier. This is consistent with prior findings that high-capacity methods trade undetectability for payload capacity [2]. Potential mitiga- tions include: (1) adversarial training with a discriminator to regularize the perturbation distribution; (2) adding an explicit steganographic loss that penalizes statistical distinguishability; or (3) reducing the per-cell perturbation amplitude at the cost of lower robustness.
- FID as a Distributional Metric
The FID of 13.7 between clean and stego distributions indicates that while individual image pairs show high PSNR and SSIM, the populations exhibit a measurable distributional shift. This is consistent with the steganalysis results: the systematic perturbation patterns shift the statistical properties of the stego distribution relative to the clean distribution, even though per- image distortion is small.
- Orthogonality Regularization
The orthogonality loss  ortho decreases from 8,728 at initial- ization to 11.4 at convergence, indicating that the 4 learned patterns within each cell evolve toward mutual orthogonality. This regularization is essential for multi-bit cells: without it, patterns may collapse into similar directions, reducing the effective per-bit signal strength and increasing BER.

- Limitations and Future Work
- Crop Robustness
The spatial tiling architecture is inherently vulnerable to crop- and-resize attacks due to spatial misalignment. Future work could address this through: (1) redundant encoding, where each bit is embedded in multiple non-adjacent cells; (2) global encoding with a learned spatial transformer that normalizes
the input before extraction; or (3) frequency-domain encoding that distributes information across spatial frequencies rather than fixed grid positions.

- Statistical Undetectability
The perfect steganalysis detection (AUC = 1.0) represents the most significant practical limitation. Incorporating adversarial training with a binary classifier or adapting cover-source mismatch mitigation techniques from classical steganography could reduce detectability, though likely at the expense of capacity or quality.
- Error-Correcting Codes
Integrating error-correcting codes (e.g., Reed-Solomon, LDPC) could improve resilience against attacks that cause partial bit errors, at the expense of reduced raw capacity. A differentiable ECC layer could be trained end-to-end with the steganographic pipeline.

- Ethical Considerations
Steganography is inherently a dual-use technology. While it has legitimate applications in digital watermarking, copyright protection, covert communication for journalism and activism, and secure data transmission, it can also be misused for covert exfiltration of sensitive data or evasion of content moderation.
We acknowledge this dual-use nature and advocate for responsible deployment:
- We commit to releasing code in a controlled manner, potentially with capacity-limited demonstrations.
- We demonstrate that differentiable attack training in- herently provides a blueprint for building steganalysis detectors, as the same attack layer can be repurposed for detection training.
- We encourage the development of corresponding ste- ganalysis methods and support their publication alongside steganographic advances.
- We note that forensic analysis of diffusion-generated images is an active area of research [4], and DSCSP’s perturbation patterns may be detectable by sufficiently powerful classifiers.

- Conclusion
We presented DSCSP, a novel steganographic framework that encodes secret messages into the sampling output of frozen pre-trained diffusion models via learned spatial perturbation patterns. Our multi-bit spatial tiling Controller achieves a 256- bit payload capacity with only 20K trainable parameters, while a spatially-aligned ResNet-18 Extractor recovers bits with perfect accuracy (BER = 0.0000) at 36.7 dB PSNR, 0.902 SSIM, 0.032 LPIPS, and FID 13.7.
Through a three-phase robustness curriculum using five differentiable attack proxies, DSCSP learns to resist Gaussian noise (BER = 0.0000 across all tested σ), blur (BER < 0.01 up to radius 1.5), brightness/contrast adjustments (BER < 0.003), and real JPEG compression at Q 90 (BER < 0.01), while identifying crop-and-resize and aggressive JPEG (Q < 70) as remaining challenges. Capacity scaling experiments demon- strate the framework’s flexibility from 128 to 512 bits, and an information-theoretic analysis provides insight into how the system exceeds classical channel capacity bounds through learned non-linear decoding.
Steganalysis evaluation reveals a key limitation: stego images are trivially detectable by a trained classifier (AUC = 1.0), highlighting the fundamental tension between payload capacity and statistical undetectability that future work must address.
The entire system trains in approximately 100 minutes on a 6 GB laptop GPU, demonstrating that high-capacity, robust generative steganography is achievable with modest computational resources. DSCSP establishes sampling-path encoding as a viable and efficient paradigm for diffusion-based steganography, opening directions for future work in adversarial undetectability training, improved JPEG proxies, crop-resilient encoding, and scaling to higher resolutions.
