Frequently Asked Questions
How should I interpret Turnitin's AI writing percentage and false positives?
The percentage shown in the AI writing report is the amount of qualifying text within the submission that Turnitin’s AI writing 
detection model determines was either likely AI-generated text from a large-language model or likely AI-generated text that was 
likely revised using an AI paraphrase tool or word spinner.
 
False positives (incorrectly flagging human-written text as AI-generated) are a possibility in AI models.
 
AI detection scores under 20%, which we do not surface in new reports, have a higher likelihood of false positives. To reduce the 
likelihood of misinterpretation, no score or highlights are attributed and are indicated with an asterisk in the report (*%).
 
The AI writing percentage should not be the sole basis to determine whether misconduct has occurred. The reviewer/instructor 
should use the percentage as a means to start a formative conversation with their student and/or use it to examine the submitted 
assignment in accordance with their school's policies.
What does 'qualifying text' mean?
Our model only processes qualifying text in the form of long-form writing. Long-form writing means individual sentences contained in paragraphs that make up a 
longer piece of written work, such as an essay, a dissertation, or an article, etc. Qualifying text that has been determined to be likely AI-generated will be 
highlighted in cyan in the submission, and likely AI-generated and then likely AI-paraphrased will be highlighted purple.
 
Non-qualifying text, such as bullet points, annotated bibliographies, etc., will not be processed and can create disparity between the submission highlights and the 
percentage shown.

DSCSP: Diffusion-Stego with Conditional Sampling
Paths for Robust Image Steganography
Authors Omitted for Review
Abstract—We introduce DSCSP (Diffusion-Stego with Condi-
tional Sampling Paths), a novel steganographic framework that
embeds secret messages into images generated by pre-trained
diffusion models. Unlike prior diffusion-based steganography
methods that project messages into initial noise vectors or
require model fine-tuning, DSCSP employs a lightweight learned
Controllernetwork that maps payload bits to spatial perturbations
applied to the output of a frozen DDIM sampling chain,
effectively using the diffusion model’s sampling path as an
information channel. A spatially-alignedExtractornetwork based
on ResNet-18 recovers the hidden bits from the stego image.
Both networks are trained end-to-end with a composite loss
combining message reconstruction, perceptual fidelity (LPIPS),
and orthogonality regularization, augmented by a curriculum
of five differentiable attacks—Gaussian noise, Gaussian blur,
brightness/contrast adjustment, crop-and-resize, and STE-based
JPEG compression—to ensure robustness against common image
transformations. On CelebA-HQ 256×256 face images, DSCSP
achieves a 256-bit payload capacity with a clean bit error rate
(BER) of 0.0000, PSNR of 36.7 dB, SSIM of 0.902, LPIPS of 0.032,
and FID of 13.7. We further demonstrate robustness against
real (non-differentiable) JPEG compression (BER <0.001 at
Q≥95 ), Gaussian noise, blur, and brightness/contrast attacks.
Capacity scaling experiments show the framework generalizes
from 128 to 512 bits by adjusting the per-cell encoding rate. An
information-theoretic analysis frames the system as a structured
additive channel and compares achieved throughput to Shannon
capacity bounds. These results establish sampling-path encoding
as a viable paradigm for high-capacity, high-fidelity, and robust
generative steganography.
Index Terms—Steganography, diffusion models, deep learning,
image security, robustness
I. INTRODUCTION
Steganography, the art of hiding information within
innocuous-looking cover media, has historically relied on
embedding data into existing images through imperceptible
pixel-level modifications [5]. With the advent of powerful
generative models, a new paradigm has emerged:generative
steganography, where the cover image itself is synthesized
to carry the hidden message, eliminating the need for a pre-
existing cover and offering inherent plausible deniability.
Diffusion models [8], [16] have recently achieved state-of-
the-art image synthesis quality, making them attractive back-
bones for generative steganography. Existing diffusion-based
steganography approaches broadly fall into two categories.
Noise-projection methods, exemplified by Diffusion-Stego [18],
encode messages directly into the initial noise vector zT before
running the reverse diffusion process, achieving a training-
free approach but offering limited robustness against post-
processing attacks.Model-adaptation methods[13], [21] fine-
tune or augment the diffusion model itself, which requires
costly retraining and risks degrading the pre-trained model’s
generation quality.
We propose a fundamentally different approach.DSCSP
(Diffusion-Stego with Conditional Sampling Paths) treats the
sampling trajectoryof a frozen pre-trained diffusion model
as an information channel. Rather than modifying the model
weights or the initial noise, DSCSP learns a compactController
network that maps payload bits to spatial perturbation patterns,
which are applied to the output of the standard DDIM [16]
deterministic sampling chain. A lightweightExtractornetwork
recovers the hidden bits from the resulting stego image. Both
networks are trained end-to-end while the diffusion model
remains completely frozen, preserving its generation quality
and enabling efficient training on consumer-grade hardware.
To ensure robustness against common image transformations
encountered during transmission and storage, we introduce a
curriculum-based adversarial training strategy that gradually
exposes the system to five differentiable attack proxies—
Gaussian noise, Gaussian blur, brightness/contrast adjustment,
random crop-and-resize, and STE-based differentiable JPEG
compression.
Our contributions are as follows:
• We introducesampling-path encoding, a novel stegano-
graphic channel for diffusion models that maps bits to
learned spatial perturbation patterns applied to frozen
DDIM outputs, achieving 256-bit payloads with 0.0000
BER, 36.7 dB PSNR, 0.902 SSIM, and FID 13.7 at
256×256resolution.
• We propose amulti-bit spatial tiling Controllerwith
orthogonality-regularized patterns that encodes 4 bits
per spatial cell using only ∼20K trainable parameters,
enabling memory-efficient training on a 6 GB GPU.
• We design aspatially-aligned Extractorwith a ResNet-
18 backbone whose stride-32 feature map naturally aligns
with the Controller’s 8×8 spatial grid, ensuring coherent
bit recovery.
• We develop adifferentiable attack curriculumcom-
prising five attacks with a three-phase training schedule
that progressively builds robustness while maintaining
reconstruction accuracy.
• We provide acomprehensive experimental evaluation
including real (non-differentiable) JPEG robustness tests,
steganalysis detection experiments with a trained ResNet-
18 classifier, capacity scaling from 128 to 512 bits, a base-

II. RELATEDWORK
A. Classical Image Steganography
Traditional steganography methods embed messages by
modifying pixel values [5] or transform-domain coefficients
of existing cover images. LSB (Least Significant Bit) re-
placement [3] modifies the least significant bits of pixel
values but is easily detectable by statistical steganalysis. More
sophisticated approaches such as HUGO [14], WOW [9], and
S-UNIWARD [10] minimize a distortion function to reduce
detectability, but remain fundamentally limited by the cover
image distribution.
B. Deep Learning-Based Steganography
Neural network-based methods jointly learn encoding and
decoding. HiDDeN [23] trains an encoder–decoder pair with
a discriminator and differentiable JPEG proxy for robustness.
SteganoGAN [20] uses GANs to embed messages directly dur-
ing image generation. These methods achieve higher capacities
than classical approaches but are constrained by the quality of
their generative backbones.
C. Diffusion-Based Steganography
Diffusion-Stego [18] projects secret messages into the initial
noise vector zT of a pre-trained diffusion model using a
training-free orthogonal projection scheme. While elegant,
this approach offers limited robustness since the encoded
information is distributed globally across the noise space and
can be disrupted by spatial transformations.
StegoDiffusion [13] explores invertible diffusion architec-
tures for steganography, leveraging the bijective mapping
between image and latent spaces. LDStega [21] operates
in the latent space of latent diffusion models, achieving
practical robustness through latent-domain message embedding.
DERO [22] addresses robustness in diffusion-based watermark-
ing by jointly training a message encoder and decoder with
the diffusion model, demonstrating resilience against various
image manipulations.
DSCSP differs from all these approaches by introducing a
learned perturbation controllerthat operates on theoutputof
a completely frozen diffusion model, requiring no modification
to the generative backbone and enabling efficient training with
a compact parameterization.
D. Differentiable Image Processing for Robustness
Training steganographic systems to resist image transfor-
mations requires differentiable proxies for non-differentiable
operations. The Straight-Through Estimator (STE) [1] enables
gradient flow through quantization operations, and has been
applied to create differentiable JPEG approximations [15]. We
leverage this technique alongside differentiable implementa-
tions of blur, noise, cropping, and color transformations to
build a comprehensive attack layer for robustness training.

V. DISCUSSION
A. Sampling-Path Encoding as a Steganographic Channel
Our results demonstrate that the output of a frozen diffusion
model’s sampling chain contains sufficient representational
capacity for reliable steganography. The learned spatial per-
turbation patterns effectively create a 256-bit channel within
the sampling “path” without modifying any component of the
generative model. The rapid convergence (BER → 0 within
440 steps) suggests that the perturbation–extraction mapping is
inherently learnable given the architectural alignment between
Controller and Extractor.
B. Quality–Robustness Tradeoff
The training dynamics reveal a clear tradeoff: introducing
attacks reduces PSNR from a peak of 37.6 dB (step 4,000, 10%
attack rate) to 35.0 dB (step 7,000, 40% attack rate), as the
Controller must embed stronger, more resilient patterns. During
Phase 3, the cosine LR schedule enables gradual refinement,
recovering PSNR to 36.7 dB while maintaining robustness—a
net loss of only∼1 dB from the peak clean PSNR.
C. STE Proxy Limitations
The real JPEG evaluation reveals a significant gap between
the differentiable STE proxy and actual JPEG codecs. While

explore more faithful differentiable JPEG approximations, such
as learned JPEG proxies trained on actual codec input–output

D. Detectability and the Capacity–Undetectability Tradeoff
The perfect steganalysis detection (AUC = 1.0) highlights a
fundamental tension in high-capacity steganography. The Con-
troller’s spatial tiling creates structured perturbation patterns
that, while imperceptible to the human eye (PSNR 36.7 dB,
SSIM 0.902), are easily distinguishable by a learned classifier.
This is consistent with prior findings that high-capacity methods
trade undetectability for payload capacity [2]. Potential mitiga-
tions include: (1) adversarial training with a discriminator to
regularize the perturbation distribution; (2) adding an explicit
steganographic loss that penalizes statistical distinguishability;
or (3) reducing the per-cell perturbation amplitude at the cost
of lower robustness.
E. FID as a Distributional Metric
The FID of 13.7 between clean and stego distributions
indicates that while individual image pairs show high PSNR and
SSIM, the populations exhibit a measurable distributional shift.
This is consistent with the steganalysis results: the systematic
perturbation patterns shift the statistical properties of the stego
distribution relative to the clean distribution, even though per-
image distortion is small.
F . Orthogonality Regularization
The orthogonality loss Lortho decreases from 8,728 at initial-
ization to 11.4 at convergence, indicating that the 4 learned
patterns within each cell evolve toward mutual orthogonality.
This regularization is essential for multi-bit cells: without it,
patterns may collapse into similar directions, reducing the
effective per-bit signal strength and increasing BER.
G. Crop Vulnerability
The spatial tiling architecture’s vulnerability to crop attacks
(BER ∼0.45 ) represents a fundamental limitation rather than
a training deficiency. When the image is cropped, the spatial
correspondence between encoded cells and Extractor feature
map positions is disrupted. This is analogous to how classical
watermarking schemes based on spatial templates suffer from
geometric attacks. We discuss potential mitigation strategies
in Section VI.
VI. LIMITATIONS ANDFUTUREWORK
A. Crop Robustness
The spatial tiling architecture is inherently vulnerable to crop-
and-resize attacks due to spatial misalignment. Future work
could address this through: (1) redundant encoding, where
each bit is embedded in multiple non-adjacent cells; (2) global
encoding with a learned spatial transformer that normalizes
the input before extraction; or (3) frequency-domain encoding
that distributes information across spatial frequencies rather
than fixed grid positions.
B. Real JPEG Robustness
While the STE-based differentiable JPEG proxy enables
gradient-based training, our evaluation reveals a substantial
gap at low quality factors (Q≤70 ). More faithful differentiable
JPEG proxies—potentially learned from codec input–output
pairs—could close this gap and extend robustness to more
aggressive compression.
C. Statistical Undetectability
The perfect steganalysis detection (AUC = 1.0) represents the
most significant practical limitation. Incorporating adversarial
training with a binary classifier or adapting cover-source
mismatch mitigation techniques from classical steganography
could reduce detectability, though likely at the expense of
capacity or quality.
D. Error-Correcting Codes
Integrating error-correcting codes (e.g., Reed-Solomon,
LDPC) could improve resilience against attacks that cause
partial bit errors, at the expense of reduced raw capacity. A
differentiable ECC layer could be trained end-to-end with the
steganographic pipeline.
E. Scaling
We demonstrate results at 256×256 resolution with 128–
512 bits. Scaling to higher resolutions (e.g., 512×512 )
with proportionally larger grids, or adapting DSCSP to latent
diffusion models (e.g., Stable Diffusion), would enable larger
payloads and more practical image resolutions.
VII. ETHICALCONSIDERATIONS
Steganography is inherently a dual-use technology. While it
has legitimate applications in digital watermarking, copyright
protection, covert communication for journalism and activism,
and secure data transmission, it can also be misused for covert
exfiltration of sensitive data or evasion of content moderation.
We acknowledge this dual-use nature and advocate for
responsible deployment:
• We commit to releasing code in a controlled manner,
potentially with capacity-limited demonstrations.
• We demonstrate that differentiable attack training in-
herently provides a blueprint for building steganalysis
detectors, as the same attack layer can be repurposed for
detection training.
• We encourage the development of corresponding ste-
ganalysis methods and support their publication alongside
steganographic advances.
• We note that forensic analysis of diffusion-generated
images is an active area of research [4], and DSCSP’s
perturbation patterns may be detectable by sufficiently
powerful classifiers.

VIII. CONCLUSION
We presented DSCSP, a novel steganographic framework
that encodes secret messages into the sampling output of frozen
pre-trained diffusion models via learned spatial perturbation
patterns. Our multi-bit spatial tiling Controller achieves a 256-
bit payload capacity with only ∼20K trainable parameters,
while a spatially-aligned ResNet-18 Extractor recovers bits
with perfect accuracy (BER = 0.0000) at 36.7 dB PSNR, 0.902
SSIM, 0.032 LPIPS, and FID 13.7.
Through a three-phase robustness curriculum using five
differentiable attack proxies, DSCSP learns to resist Gaussian
noise (BER = 0.0000 across all tested σ), blur (BER <0.01 up
to radius 1.5), brightness/contrast adjustments (BER <0.003 ),
and real JPEG compression at Q≥90 (BER <0.01 ), while
identifying crop-and-resize and aggressive JPEG ( Q <70 ) as
remaining challenges. Capacity scaling experiments demon-
strate the framework’s flexibility from 128 to 512 bits, and
an information-theoretic analysis provides insight into how
the system exceeds classical channel capacity bounds through
learned non-linear decoding.
Steganalysis evaluation reveals a key limitation: stego images
are trivially detectable by a trained classifier (AUC = 1.0),
highlighting the fundamental tension between payload capacity
and statistical undetectability that future work must address.
The entire system trains in approximately 100 minutes
on a 6 GB laptop GPU, demonstrating that high-capacity,
robust generative steganography is achievable with modest
computational resources. DSCSP establishes sampling-path
encoding as a viable and efficient paradigm for diffusion-based
steganography, opening directions for future work in adversarial
undetectability training, improved JPEG proxies, crop-resilient
encoding, and scaling to higher resolutions.
